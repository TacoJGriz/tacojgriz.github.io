If you know me, you probably know by now that coding is not the only computer-sciency thing I'm interested in. In recent years I have developed an interest in Linux and self-hosting, so today I wanna take some time to talk about my self hosting adventures!

---

I've been interested in self-hosting for quite some time, however I never actually started a server because I thought that acquiring the appropriate hardware would be expensive. If I'm being honest, what caused me to actually get started was watching [PewDiePie's video on degoogleing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u_Lxkt50xOg&t=636s) where he talks about self-hosting. I saw he was using his Steam deck to self host, and I decided if he could run a server on such an impractical device, then I could figure something out. I quickly flashed an SD card with Raspberry Pi OS an popped it into my Raspberry Pi 3B and away I was!

### But why did I want to get into self-hosting anyways? There's a few reasons

Long story short: Google. Ever since I learned that big companies take my data and sell it, I've never really felt comfortable. It’s one thing to promise privacy, but when all is said and done, I don't know how much of my data Google and other big companies are actually keeping private and what's being looked at and sold. I don't want to be tracked by them either. Sure, using user data can be beneficial for some services (i.e., Maps), but truth be told I just like feeling in control. It’s the same reason why I use Linux; it feels good being in charge of my technology.

Another big thing for me is owning vs streaming my media. I've never been a huge fan of subscription-based services. I have no reason to pay a bagillion dollars a month to Netflix whenever I have only a handful of shows and movies I like to watch, and half of them are on other random streaming services anyways. There’s also the fact that any given moment, Netflix can decide to remove a show or Prime Video will put something behind ads or a paywall or etc... Nonetheless, I prefer owning than streaming. My small collection of favorites is all I need to be happy. The only exception is music. I really like exploring new music, so I feel like I need to keep using YouTube Music just so I can keep discovering. When I really like an album, I'll buy it and download it to my library so I can support the artist. This hybrid streaming and buying approach has worked well for me thus far.

### Nonetheless, back to my naive self with my Raspberry Pi and very little Docker experience

The plan was to use my Raspberry Pi in order to run a very minimal server, just to get started with Nextcloud. Things went okay! Like all things I do, much troubleshooting was required but I started typing `docker` commands and away I was.

The first thing I did was set up Portainer. For my level of experience, portainer was lifesaving! For those who don’t know what Portainer is, it lets you visually manage all of your Docker containers from anywhere you can access your server. As I navigated environmental variables, volumes, and restart policies, Portainer made it so easy to figure out how it all worked. In retrospect, working with Portainer has given me such a better understanding of how to use Docker.

Setting up Nextcloud was easy. It took me a minute to find the right image for my Pi but one `docker` container later and everything was working the way it was supposed to. Goodbye Google Drive!

Wrong! I still had a long way to go before I was ready to kiss goodbye all of the precious services I had been stuck using. I still couldn't access my server from anywhere other than my house, and that simply would not do.

### How do you access a server from anywhere else?

There’s a few ways! But one thing is certain: I wanted to be safe about it and I totally wanted my own domain name. Sure I could have forwarded every single port I needed and memorized all of the arbitrary numbers that are my IP and ports, but that wasn’t very safe or easy. The solution was NGINX proxy manager. All traffic goes through two ports. I quickly set up NGINX, found a domain name I liked, and before I knew it, I could access my Nextcloud from anywhere.

### What comes next?

More storage. I found an unused HDD, bought a cheap SATA to USB interface, and connected it. Due to the Raspberry Pi’s limited space, this would be important if I wanted to put more media on my server. I created a mount point and got Plex setup, but little did I know, this was the end of the beginning.

Everything was working well until it wasn’t. My first gripe was that Plex making me pay to access my library remotely, and that didn’t sit right with me. Why would I have to pay to access media I own? I didn't have long to consider my options though because before I knew it, everything went down. A quick SSH in and I realized that the Pi wasn't detecting my external hard drive; an issue I would need to fix in Austin while I was stuck in Houston. The school semester ended and I finally had time to figure out what was the matter. After some quick experimenting, I figured out that the external hard drive did not automatically turn back on after losing power the way my Pi did. I decided it was time to ditch the setup. It was great for a first server but offered inconsistent performance, and it was often times annoying trying to find images compatible with the Pi’s ARMv7 architecture.

### Fortunately, I already had something else brewing

I have a gaming PC that lives at home. However, I’m not a huge fan of subscription-based services like Netflix or Prime Video. A lot of shows and movies are on other random streaming services anyway. Nonetheless, I prefer owning than streaming. My small collection of favorites is all I need to be happy.

First, in order to avoid needing to port forward at all, I set up Cloudflare Tunnels instead of using NGINX again. Cloudflare tunnels have worked great for me and I like feeling like my home network is at least a little more secure. Secondly, I am now using Jellyfin as my media server. Jellyfin is free and open source, which is much more appealing to me than paying for Plex.

My latest endeavour has been self-hosting AI. Recently, PewDiePie released Odysseus, a FOSS self-hosted AI workspace. Between this and finally having a server with a GPU and capable components, self hosting AI felt like an achievable goal. I determined that Qwen2.5:7b would run the best on my hardware, which is not the most advanced model but will be sufficient for my use. I set up Ollama and downloaded the model. Setting up both Ollama and Odysseus was a breeze. I plan to train my own model eventually.

### Why should you self-host AI?

As cool as AI has gotten recently, there are also plenty of downsides to this newly popular technology. Apart from wanting to keep my data private, as mentioned earlier, I’m also concerned about big data centers being built to run and train AI models. These centers can consume a lot of energy and be very polluting, and if I want to use AI, I would rather run it myself on a setup I can control and deem morally acceptable.

The reason why I want to train my own model is because a lot of companies don't care about what data they are using to train their models. When I train my own model, I’d like to only use data that is publicly available to use so I don’t feel like my model is plagiarizing from people who don't want to be plagiarized.

### So what's next?

I am pretty happy with where my setup is right now. The main thing I want to try to add in the future is host my photos as well, maybe through Immich. I might also host a password manager through Bitwarden. These however, are projects for later.

I've had a lot of fun working on this setup. I'm definitively the tinker-er type of person and this project has very much satisfied my need to tinker. I love feeling like I have control of my data, media, and the services I use, and can't wait to see what I self-host next.
